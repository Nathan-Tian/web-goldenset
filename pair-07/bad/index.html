Prologue · 序章

Where the desert keeps its memory

Chapter 00 — The Threshold

Deep in the Gobi Desert, at the western edge of the Hexi Corridor, an oasis town once guarded the meeting point of empires. Its name — Dunhuang, “blazing beacon” — promised travelers that water, shelter, and safety lay ahead after weeks of open sand. Traders from Chang’an and Kashgar, monks from India, envoys from Persia, and musicians from Samarkand all paused here beneath the same cliffs.

Between the 4th and 14th centuries, those travelers gave the cliffs a gift: cave after cave hollowed from conglomerate rock, their walls dressed in clay and painted with mineral pigments — azurite skies, malachite hills, cinnabar robes, and gold leaf haloes. What began as a single hermit’s cave grew into the Mogao Caves, the largest treasury of Buddhist art on earth, and a painted encyclopedia of the Silk Road itself.

This exhibition is a digital pilgrimage. No plane ticket, no 45-minute flight to the desert’s edge — just a scroll through light, line, and color that has outlived dynasties. We cannot bring the caves to you; so we bring their lantern-light to the screen, one mural at a time.

The Mogao Caves · 莫高窟

Nine stories rising from the sand

Chapter 01 — The Cliff of a Thousand Buddhas

The Mogao Caves

Local legend tells of the monk Lezun, who in 366 CE saw a golden light shining from the cliff at the edge of the Daquan River — a vision of a thousand Buddhas. He carved the first meditation cave into the rock. Over the next ten centuries, patrons from Buddhist monks to Tang empresses, from oasis merchants to military governors, added hundreds more.

Today the site we call Mogao — “Peerless” — preserves 735 caves cut into a 1.6-kilometre cliff. Inside, more than 2,400 painted sculptures and 45,000 square metres of murals survive: the world’s longest-running painted conversation between China and the cultures of Central Asia.

Gallery I · 天宫伎乐

Feitian — Heaven in Flight

飞天 · Flying Apsaras of the Ceiling

Above every cave, the ceiling dissolves into paradise. Feitian — flying celestial musicians, dancers and flower-scatterers — stream across azurite skies with scarves that loop and curl like calligraphy made of wind. No wings carry them; only trailing silk, which painters used as a living line to suggest speed, grace and weightlessness.

Inspired by the apsaras of Indian art, the Dunhuang feitian slowly grew Chinese: faces grew serene, robes grew long, and the ribbon line became a masterpiece of brushwork in its own right. More than 4,500 of them survive across the caves.

Gallery II · 本生故事

The Nine-Colored Deer — A Life Before Life

九色鹿 · Jataka Tales of the Buddha

Buddhist murals do not only show one lifetime. Jataka tales — stories of the Buddha’s past lives — turned the cave walls into a vast picture-book of compassion. In the most beloved of them, painted on the west wall of Cave 257, a deer with fur of nine colors rescues a drowning man, only to be betrayed by him for a queen’s ransom.

Read like a scroll from right to left, the story unfolds in a single continuous landscape: the rescue, the promise, the betrayal, and finally the deer’s quiet lesson before the king. It is one of the earliest and most cinematic narrative murals in Chinese art.

Gallery III · 乐舞

Music & Dance — Silk Road Rhythm

反弹琵琶 · The Dancer of Cave 112

No image is more famous from Dunhuang than the dancer of Cave 112: one leg raised, body arched, playing the pipa — a pear-shaped lute — behind her head. It is an impossible pose, a painter’s shorthand for music so divine that the laws of the body bend to meet it.

Around her, orchestras of the Tang dynasty mix instruments from across Asia: Indian flutes, Persian harps, Central Asian drums. Cave murals record more than a hundred kinds of musicians and dancers — a moving map of how the Silk Road shared not only goods, but sound.

Gallery IV · 丝路众生

Everyday Silk Road — The World in a Cave

商旅图 · Travelers, Traders & Fields

Not every mural is divine. Patrons also asked painters to record the world they knew: camel caravans crossing passes, merchants haggling in bilingual markets, farmers ploughing oasis fields, hunters on horseback, dancers at court, and ships on distant rivers. In Cave 45, a famous mural shows a Sui merchant praying before a mountain bandit — an image of the road’s real dangers.

These “secular” scenes are priceless social history. They show people with the round eyes of Sogdians, the curls of Indians, the pointed hats of Turks — proof that the caves were painted by many hands and for many peoples, at the crossroads of the world.

Gallery V · 藏经洞

The Library Cave — A Sealed Window

金刚经 · Cave 17 and Its Scrolls

In about the 11th century, someone walled up a small side cave stuffed with manuscripts — and the secret held for nine hundred years. When it was opened in 1900, Cave 17 yielded tens of thousands of scrolls, paintings, and documents in Chinese, Tibetan, Sogdian, Khotanese, Uyghur and Sanskrit.

Among them was the Diamond Sutra of 868 CE — the world’s oldest dated printed book, its woodblock pages still crisp after twelve centuries. The Library Cave turned Dunhuang into the single greatest archive of medieval Central Asian life: contracts, medical recipes, poems, school exercises, and prayers written by people whose voices history had otherwise lost.

The Long Road · 千年之路

A thousand years, layer by layer

Chapter 02 — Historical Context
c. 139–119 BCE

The road opens

Imperial envoy Zhang Qian travels west, and the Han dynasty extends its reach to Dunhuang — the “gateway” where the Silk Road splits north and south around the Taklamakan Desert.

111 BCE

Dunhuang commandery founded

The Han establishes a frontier garrison at Dunhuang, guarding the throat of trade between China, Central Asia and beyond.

c. 366 CE

A vision in the cliff

According to legend, the monk Lezun sees golden light over the cliffs of the Daquan River and carves the first cave. The Mogao Caves are born.

386–581 CE

Northern dynasties

Monks and local rulers add caves in austere, stylized styles. The earliest surviving murals blend Indian, Central Asian and Chinese line.

618–907 CE

Tang golden age

Dunhuang becomes a cosmopolitan crossroads. The grandest caves — with towering Buddhas, palace-scale processions and feitian-filled ceilings — date to this era.

c. 781–848 CE

Tibetan rule

The region falls under Tibetan control; murals blend Tibetan and Chinese styles, and Tibetan manuscripts enter the caves.

c. 11th century

The cave is sealed

For reasons still debated — perhaps war, perhaps renewal — Cave 17 is walled shut, preserving its treasures untouched for centuries.

1900

A wall falls

Daoist caretaker Wang Yuanlu clears sand from a cracked wall and discovers the Library Cave. Its contents soon travel to collections across the world.

1944

Protection begins

China establishes the Dunhuang Art Research Institute — later the Dunhuang Academy — under pioneering painter Chang Shuhong to study and guard the caves.

1987

World Heritage

The Mogao Caves are inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognized as “the largest, most richly endowed, and longest-used treasure house of Buddhist art in the world.”

Today

The digital cave

High-resolution scanning, 3-D modeling and global digitization projects — “Digital Dunhuang” — are building a virtual twin of the caves, so the art may survive climate, time, and even the pressure of our footsteps.

Pigments & Craft · 丹青妙手

Colors ground from the earth

Long before tubes of paint, Dunhuang artists ground stones into color. Every hue came from the desert and the trade routes — which is why the walls still glow.
Azurite Blue石青 — from mineral stone
Malachite Green石绿 — copper carbonate
Cinnabar Red朱砂 — mercury sulfide
Gold Leaf贴金 — beaten into halo
Ochre赭石 — iron-rich earth

The method was patient. First, artisans mixed desert clay and straw into a plaster skin over the rough stone. Then painters sketched with dilute ochre — the confident “flying white” brush line that later Chinese art would perfect. On top went mineral pigments, each crushed, washed, and bound with animal glue.

Because mineral particles reflect light, Dunhuang colors have a strange life: they seem lit from within. Cinnabar darkened by centuries to a deep brown; azurite sometimes blackened; but gold leaf still catches the flashlight of every visitor like the day it was laid down.

The palette itself is a trade map — lapis and vermilion arrived along the very roads the murals depict, turning every cave into a small museum of the Silk Road in minerals.

Why Digital Matters · 守望千年
Chapter 03 — Preservation

Every year, the murals fade a little more.
Every pixel we save, they stay a little longer.

The caves face quiet enemies: salt that crystallizes behind painted surfaces, humidity from human breath, light, dust, and the slow chemistry of age. Even the most careful visitor — one of 20,000 allowed on peak days — brings warmth that stresses the walls. This is why the world’s great heritage institutions turned to light itself: to photograph, scan, and model the caves before it is too late.

Visit respectfully

Book through official channels, never touch the walls, never use flash — the paint is more fragile than it looks.

Share digital first

Prefer high-resolution archives and virtual tours over crowding fragile caves. Digital visits protect the originals.

Tell the story

Heritage survives when it is loved. Share what you learn here — awareness is the cheapest preservation tool.

Plan Your Visit · 参访指南

Come to the cliff, or let it come to you

The Mogao Caves sit at the edge of the Taklamakan Desert, 25 km southeast of Dunhuang city in Gansu Province, China. Whether you travel in person or through the digital archives below, here is how to begin.

Getting thereDunhuang & Mogao

Fly or take the high-speed train to Dunhuang (via Lanzhou or Xi’an). From the city, shuttle buses run to the Mogao Grottoes Visitor Center, where all tours begin — private cars stop at the gate.

Season · best spring & autumn

Tickets & rulesInside the caves

Admission is by timed guided tour only; you will visit a rotating selection of caves, never all 735. Photography is not permitted inside, bags stay in lockers, and silence protects both acoustics and atmosphere.

Book · official visitor center

Without travelingThe digital cave

High-resolution virtual tours, gigapixel murals and 3-D cave models are published by the Dunhuang Academy’s “Digital Dunhuang” project — explore dozens of caves from your own desk, in detail sharper than the human eye.

Explore · digitaldunhuang.china

A gentle warning from the caves: Dunhuang is a real desert. In summer the sand can pass 40°C and in winter drop below −10°C. Bring water, sun protection, and comfortable shoes — and remember that the murals you are allowed to see are among the most carefully guarded art on Earth. Treat them like the treasures they are.
